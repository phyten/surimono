Measuring Folded Card Spacers for Model Rails
Anonymous CVPR submission
Abstract
A folded card spacer fixes the height of a model rail above
its baseboard. We measured spacers folded from three card
stocks in a single test slot and compared the measured
heights with a simple layer model. The model predicts the
height of a folded spacer to within a tenth of a millimetre
when the crease allowance is taken from a two-layer trial.
We describe the slot, the measurement procedure and the
remaining sources of error, and we list practical rules for
choosing a card stock for a given rail height.

2. Method
2.1. The test slot
The slot was cut into a block of laminated board and lined
with a strip of thin brass so that the card could not dent the
walls. A sliding rail of known height rests on top of the
spacer, and the height is read from a scale on the side of
the block. The block was clamped to the bench throughout
the trial so that the scale did not move between readings.
Each spacer was inserted with its folded edge towards
the back of the slot. The rail was lowered onto it and left
for ten seconds before the reading was taken, because a
freshly folded spacer springs back slowly. Three readings
were taken for each spacer, removing and reinserting it
between readings.
2.2. Layer model
Paper models of railways need many small parts of exact
height. Rails, platform edges and signal bases all sit on
spacers, and a spacer that is too thick by a fraction of a
millimetre lifts a rail joint enough to derail a light wagon.
Most builders cut spacers from card and fold them until
they reach the height they want [3]. Folding is quick and
needs no tools, but the height of a folded spacer is not sim-
ply the sum of the card thicknesses: each crease adds a
small allowance, and the allowance depends on the card
and on how firmly the crease is pressed.
This note reports a bench trial of folded spacers. Our
aims were:
We model the height h of a spacer with n layers of card of
thickness t as
h= n t + (n− 1) c,
(1)
where c is the crease allowance added by each fold. The
allowance is found from a two-layer trial, for which Equa-
tion (1) gives c = h2− 2t. The same model was used for
the gauge block of Scale Fixture Room [4], where it pre-
dicted the height of folded board well for up to four layers.

The model agrees with the measurements to within
0.06 mm for every spacer. The largest error is for the thick
stock, where the crease allowance grows with the number
of layers: the outer layers must bend around a thicker core,
and the crease opens slightly. For the thin and medium
stocks the error does not grow with the number of layers,
which agrees with the note of Glue Pot Corner [2] on thin
paper joints.
Repeated readings of the same spacer differed by at
most 0.02 mm. Spacers that were left in the slot overnight
lost about 0.01 mm of height, which is smaller than the
spread between readings and was ignored.
4. Conclusion
A folded card spacer can be designed from the card thick-
ness and a crease allowance measured once for each stock.
For heights up to 2 mm the thin and medium stocks follow
the layer model closely, and they are the better choice
when a rail must sit at an exact height. The thick stock is
quicker to fold but should be checked in the slot when
more than three layers are used. A card ladder [5] gives a
convenient way to check many spacers at once.
