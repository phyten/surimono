Following the continuous rail
At the inlet, the team repeated the check. The reader carried the rule from
the outer shelf to the slot at the centre of the board. At the first station the
front edge rested against a shallow stop. A pencil line showed how far the
tongue could move before the spring began to bend. Turning the strip over
exposed a second line that had been hidden by the lip. The clerk entered both
marks in the same box and drew a short bar between them. A narrow gap
beneath the board let the strip settle after each reading. When the gauge re-
turned to the shelf the lower mark remained visible. The last entry records
the position of the stop after the board was lifted. At the first bend the nar-
row foot of the rule touched the curved wall, so the team took the reading
from the opposite bank. This moved the observer without moving the gauge.
A second slip carried a sketch of the foot beside the curve. At the middle gate
there was no room for the full rule. The shorter rule had the same divisions
but started at its cut end. The clerk copied the offset from the sketch before
entering the next value. Beyond the gate the shelf widened. Here both rules
could lie flat, and the difference between their readings matched the offset on
the slip. On the return journey the team left the gauge in place and changed
only the direction from which the marks were read. The two entries agreed at
the straight stations. At the bend they differed by half a division, the width
of the pencil line itself. The final drawing therefore shows a band around the
curve and a single stroke along the straight reach. A note inside the cover
identifies the short rule so the next crew can repeat the check with the same
pair of tools.
